FAMILY DIGITAL SAFETY AUDIT

UK Edition  |  15 Questions  |  Takes 3 Minutes

Most parents I speak to assume their family is safer online than they actually are. This isn't a catch-out. It's a quick, honest check, because the gaps between what we think we've covered and what we have covered is exactly where the risks hide. Answer each question as honestly as you can.

Section 1 of 5
Online Communication & Contact
Question 1
Does your child know they should never share personal details online, including their real name, school, location, or phone number?
Why this matters: A predator doesn't need much. A school name and a first name is often enough to find a child in real life.
Question 2
Do you know who your child is talking to online, across all the platforms and games they use?
Why this matters: Most grooming starts in the comment sections of games and apps, not on dedicated chat platforms. You will never know who they are really talking to, even if they think they do.
Question 3
Has your child been taught what to do if someone online makes them feel uncomfortable, pressured, or scared?
Why this matters: Children who don't have a plan, and permission to come to you without fear of punishment, are far less likely to tell someone when something goes wrong.
📱
Section 2 of 5
Platform & App Safety
Question 4
Are your child's social media profiles set to private, so only approved followers can see their content?
Why this matters: A public profile is a public advertisement. Under the Online Safety Act, platforms must now offer stronger defaults for under-18s, but they don't always apply them automatically.
Question 5
Do you know the actual age requirements for the platforms and apps your child uses, and whether they meet them?
Why this matters: TikTok, Instagram, and Snapchat all require users to be 13 or over. Many children lie about their age to access them, and the platform content is not designed with younger children in mind.
Question 6
Have you checked and adjusted the privacy and safety settings in the apps and games your child uses most?
Why this matters: Default settings are rarely the safest settings. Direct messaging, location sharing, and public profiles are often switched on by default.
🔐
Section 3 of 5
Device & Account Security
Question 7
Have you set up parental controls on your child's devices, including phones, tablets, gaming consoles, and smart TVs?
Why this matters: Parental controls aren't surveillance. They're guardrails, particularly important for younger children who haven't yet developed the judgment to navigate everything the internet contains.
Question 8
Do family devices have screen locks, and does your child have a unique password for their important accounts?
Why this matters: An unlocked device in the wrong hands, or a reused password after a data breach, can open the door to a lot of harm very quickly.
Question 9
Do devices charge outside your child's bedroom at night?
Why this matters: Unsupervised late-night screen time is one of the biggest risk factors for both poor sleep and exposure to harmful content. It's one of the simplest safeguards, and one of the most effective.
🧠
Section 4 of 5
Content & Critical Thinking
Question 10
Has your child been taught how to spot fake profiles, manipulative messages, and phishing attempts?
Why this matters: AI now makes it possible to generate a convincing fake person, complete with photos, voice, and video. Children need to know that what they see online is not always real.
Question 11
Have you talked with your child about deepfakes, AI-generated images, and how realistic fake content can be?
Why this matters: Non-consensual intimate image abuse, including AI-generated images, is now a criminal offence under UK law. Children need to understand both the risks to themselves and the legal consequences of sharing such content.
Question 12
Does your child know how to report harmful content or block someone who is making them feel unsafe online?
Why this matters: Knowing how to remove themselves from a threatening situation, and report it, gives children real agency. That confidence is one of the most protective things you can give them.
🏠
Section 5 of 5
Family Rules & Open Conversation
Question 13
Does your family have clear, agreed rules around screen time, which apps can be used, and what is and isn't acceptable online?
Why this matters: Clear expectations remove ambiguity. Children are much less likely to push into unsafe territory when they understand the boundaries and why they exist.
Question 14
Do you review your child's online activity together at least monthly, not as a check-up, but as an open conversation?
Why this matters: Regular, non-judgemental check-ins build trust. When children know they won't be punished for telling you something difficult, they are far more likely to come to you before a situation escalates.
Question 15
Does your child feel genuinely comfortable coming to you if something goes wrong or upsets them online, without fear of having their device taken away?
Why this matters: This is the most important question on this whole list. Open, honest, non-judgemental communication is the single most protective thing a parent can build. Everything else on here matters, but this matters most.
