Is Locket Widget safe for kids?

By Dale, Cyber Safety Guy (former RAF Police digital forensics investigator) · Updated November 2026

Locket is the app parents are often told is the "safe" alternative to Snapchat. In a lot of ways it is safer. But "safe-looking" and "safe" aren't always the same thing, and Locket is a good example of why.

The short answer

Safer than most social apps, but not risk-free. There's no public feed, no likes and no strangers' content. The risks are quieter: photos appear on your child's home screen instantly with no preview, they can't be unsent, and Locket's terms give the company a broad licence to use what's shared. It isn't a grooming platform. It's a privacy and permanence problem.

What is Locket Widget?

Locket is a free photo-sharing app that lives in a widget on your phone's home screen. It launched in 2022, built by a developer who wanted an easy way to share pictures with his girlfriend while they were apart.

Your child adds friends, up to 20 on the free version (a paid subscription removes the limit). When one of them takes a photo in the app, it appears straight away on everyone else's home screen. There's no notification to tap and no feed to scroll. It's just there.

It's very popular with teenagers, with more than 10 million downloads on Android alone. The App Store rates it 12+.

What parents should know

There's no preview and no way to decline

A photo doesn't wait in an inbox to be opened. It lands on the home screen the moment it's sent, so whatever's in it, your child sees it instantly, with no chance to brace for it or choose not to look.

There's no unsend

Once a photo has been sent to a friend, Locket says it can't be deleted from the friend's phone. Friends can also screenshot it and share it anywhere.

The terms let Locket reuse photos

By using the app you give Locket a broad, worldwide licence to host, store, copy and share what's uploaded. Users keep "ownership", but ownership and control aren't the same thing once you've handed over a licence like that. I have no evidence Locket misuses children's photos. But Common Sense Media's privacy programme, which reviews apps for children's data protection, gave Locket a "Warning" rating, citing those content rights, data shared with third parties for advertising and no real way for parents to step in.

The friend list is the audience

Twenty people sounds small, but one "friend of a friend" add is all it takes for someone your child doesn't really know to see a photo the second it lands. Backgrounds, school uniforms and daily routines can also give away more than your child realises over time.

Why it matters more because it looks safe

I hear it from parents all the time: "At least it's not Snapchat." "At least there's no algorithm." All of that can be true, and the app can still be building a permanent, licensed archive of your child's private photos that they'll never fully take back. Both things are true at once, and that's the trap.

What I'd do as a parent

I wouldn't delete it. For lots of families Locket is a better choice than the alternatives. Instead, I'd have a few specific conversations.

Go through the friend list together

Not as an inspection, as a chat: "Remind me how you know this person?" If your child can't answer easily, that's the moment to talk about removing them, together.

Talk about permanence before it's needed

Before sending anything, picture it staying exactly where it lands, forever, uneditable and undeletable, seen by a company you've never met as well as the friend it was meant for. If a photo doesn't survive that thought, it doesn't get sent. That's far more useful than "don't send nudes", which every teenager has heard and tuned out.

Agree what happens if something arrives that shouldn't

Because there's no preview, your child needs to know in advance that if a friend sends something inappropriate, it isn't their fault, and they should tell you or another trusted adult straight away.

Some questions that help:

  • "Who's on your Locket? How do you know them?"
  • "Could someone work out where you are from your photos?"
  • "Would you still send it if a teacher or a future employer saw it?"
  • "How would you feel if someone screenshotted it?"

For teachers and DSLs

If Locket comes up in a safeguarding conversation, the pattern to listen for is "no preview, no undo, broad content rights". That combination applies to a growing number of smaller apps, so it's worth teaching as a general principle in digital literacy sessions. There'll be another Locket next year with a different name.

Common questions

What is the Locket app used for?

Sharing photos with a small group of close friends. Each photo appears straight away on the friends' phone home screens in a widget.

Can you delete a photo on Locket?

Not from your friends' phones. Once a photo has been sent, Locket says it can't be removed from the receiving device, and friends can screenshot it.

What age is Locket for?

The App Store rates it 12+. Whatever their age, it's worth going through the friend list and the "picture it forever" rule together.

Can strangers see my child's Locket photos?

Only people on their friend list see them, so the risk depends on who they've added. That's why the friend list conversation matters most.
